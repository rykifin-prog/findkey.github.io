The Floor Just Collapsed

Three kids in my house shipped video games this month. None of them can write a line of code. The youngest is six.

The dominant story about AI right now is that it is making us dumber. That it offloads thinking, erodes craft, and produces a generation of people who outsource the hard parts and atrophy on contact.

I want to offer the opposite reading.

Over the last few weeks, three kids in my house each built a video game. They designed the mechanics. They named the characters. They chose the rules. They iterated until each game felt the way they wanted it to feel. None of them can write a line of code. They worked entirely through prompts.

The games are below. Play them.

Michael's game

Michael built a sandbox. There is no goal. You place blocks. You can spawn aliens. You can detonate TNT. There is a day-night cycle. There are whale sharks and shooting stars and a character he named Doofus.

It is not a game in the traditional sense. It is a tool for making things. He built a creative environment, then released it into the world and told us to figure out what to do with it.

What I noticed

Three kids, three completely different games. That is the part that surprised me.

I had assumed, before this experiment, that AI tools would flatten creative output. That if you handed three children the same prompt-to-game capability, they would converge on something similar. The opposite happened. Given a tool that removed the friction of building, each kid revealed what they actually thought a game was for.

Michael wanted a creative environment with no rules. Dagny wanted a world to explore. Sofia wanted a cast of characters with strong opinions about who can and cannot be eaten.

The AI did not make them creative. It made them legible. It collapsed the gap between what was already in their heads and what existed in the world. For most of human history that gap was the entire game. You had an idea, and then you spent years acquiring the skills to ship it, and somewhere in the middle most ideas died of attrition.

That gap is collapsing. Not gone. Collapsing. And the people who notice first will define what the next decade looks like.

The interesting question

The interesting question is not whether AI makes us lazy. It is who gets to be a builder when the cost of building approaches zero.

The kids who shipped these games will graduate into a world where the friction between idea and artifact is a fraction of what it was for any prior generation. They will not learn to code in the way I had to. They will learn to specify, to iterate, to know when something is wrong, to know when something is done. Those are different muscles, and they are the muscles that matter when the building part is cheap.

The destructive-force-of-nature reading of AI is not wrong about everything. It is wrong about the direction of the arrow. The thing that is collapsing is not human capability. It is the floor under who gets to build. And when the floor collapses, more people end up on the field.

Including, apparently, my six-year-old.
